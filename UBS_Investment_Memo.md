# UBS (UBSG): Investment memo

Four parties give different figures in this memo: UBS, the government, the analysts and me. Look at the appendix if it makes them clearer: it sets them side by side.

## The one-minute view

**Hold** · Price at rating: USD 43.32, the close of 20 November 2026 · I buy below: USD 36.73 · The two events take from a share: USD 0.60 at best, USD 3.19 on my odds, USD 6.59 at worst · Horizon: to the end of 2027

**Hold at USD 43.32. I buy below USD 36.73, the price less both events at their worst.**

My thesis is about two events that take money from UBS's shareholders.

1. The first is the AT1 lawsuit. About 3,000 holders are suing to reverse the 2023 write-off of Credit Suisse's AT1 bonds, the bonds a bank can cancel to absorb its losses. A first court found for them in 2025, the Federal Supreme Court has yet to rule, and if they win, UBS pays.
2. The second is the capital rule: parliament is deciding how much of each dollar of its foreign subsidiaries UBS's parent bank must pay for with its own equity. It is less than half today and 90 cents in the version the upper house has passed, the 90/10. UBS would pay for either out of its buybacks.

I am not predicting how either ends. I put odds on each outcome and price what it would mean for shareholders. On my odds the two take USD 3.19 from a share, about 7% of its price, and USD 6.59, about 15%, if both go against UBS. The analysts' estimates have about USD 0.2bn a year of charges for all legal cases together, and buybacks of USD 5.3bn in 2027 and USD 5.8bn in 2028.

So I hold. I do not sell. Neither event changes how UBS earns. Both take from what it pays out, and on my odds they take about one year of payouts. The verdicts on both events loom over 2027, and I think there is much uncertainty about how they end. Until I am proven wrong on them the cost stands, so I do not buy at this price. Below USD 36.73 I have a margin of safety that accounts for my worst case in full. The government's own version of the rule would cost more (Pillar 2). I do not short either: I have not forecast the market outlook or how UBS's value reacts to it, so there is no short call I can support.

The business is not my question. UBS is a good bank, and I take its earnings from the estimates 15 analysts sent UBS on 7 October 2026: profit rises from USD 11.2bn in 2026 to USD 14.6bn in 2028 as the costs of integrating Credit Suisse end. My question is whether those gains flow back to shareholders or to the two events.

The economy is the risk in my call. I do not forecast it, so I rely on the share's price for it, and the outlook for 2027 is still uncertain. If the banks fall together, my buy price falls with them. A weaker economy also makes the events cost more (The case). I have not modelled that. I watch it.

Three events will move my buy price or prove me wrong: the vote of the National Council, parliament's other chamber, expected in the winter session of 2026; UBS's first capital plan after the final vote; and the Federal Supreme Court's ruling, which has no date and which I assume comes in 2027. Each one that goes UBS's way raises my buy price.

## The business

**UBS is a good bank, not a spectacular one, and the question for its shareholders is not the business.**

**The business: nothing its competitors do not offer.** I think UBS offers nothing unique, as a universal bank or division by division. It earns 13.6% on tangible equity, eighth of the eleven banks it competes with, and spends 73% of its revenue, the most of them; no division leads its field as a whole. Its wealth management is the strong part: about half the core divisions' revenue and 45% of their profit. Part of that may be positioning. UBS wants to be seen as the leading wealth manager, and the split of revenue between its divisions is its own, unpublished, and favours wealth management; the filings do not show whether that is by design.

**Where it is genuinely strong, and where it is not.** The strength is wealth management outside the Americas: those private banks keep 37% to 46% of their revenue as profit, more than the international rivals with figures. The US business keeps 15%, the second lowest of six US wealth firms, yet it brings about half of wealth management's revenue, and the Americas a third of the group's. So the group leans on the region where it earns least.

**Diversified, but not global.** No region brings more than 35% of UBS's revenue, where the large US banks earn 51% to 86% of theirs at home. But the Americas and Switzerland together bring two thirds and Asia Pacific 16%, so UBS still rides the US and Swiss economies.

**What its earnings ride on: interest rates and the value of client assets.** The two pull against each other. UBS estimates that a rise of one point in interest rates adds about USD 1.3bn to a year's interest income, USD 1.0bn of it from franc rates. About a third of the revenue in the analysts' estimates is fees charged on the value of client assets, so a lasting fall of 10% in those values takes about USD 1.9bn a year, about a tenth of profit. In 2022, before the takeover, rates rose and markets fell together: wealth management's interest income rose by USD 1.0bn, its fees on assets fell by USD 0.9bn, and UBS's net profit rose 2%. When markets fall and rates do not rise, nothing offsets the fees.

**Credit where it is due.** It is one of the largest banks in the world by client money, USD 7.3trn, third of the eleven, on the smallest balance sheet of them, USD 1.7trn.

**The edge: discipline.** I think the edge is discipline: UBS takes risk seriously and holds buffers that make it extremely resilient beside the others, which is what one wants from a safe bank. Its CET1 ratio, shareholders' own money over its assets weighted by risk, is 14.7%, the second highest of the eleven, and its room above its own requirement, 3.9 points, the widest. Its investment bank is capped at a quarter of the group's risk. It holds a cash reserve of USD 324bn, 179% of what a month-long run would take. The resilience is in capital and cash, not in cost.

**Management is paid to keep it that way, with one qualification.** Half of the executive board's deferred shares vest on the three-year return on CET1, and half on the share's return against other global banks. A second deferred award is written down if the CET1 ratio falls below 10%, and loses a fifth for each loss-making year. Each executive must also hold a minimum of UBS shares. The qualification: a return on CET1 is easier to raise with less capital, so the pay rewards return and survival, not extra cushion. Management has also brought the bank through the Credit Suisse integration well: USD 14.6bn of an expected USD 15.1bn is spent, and the work ends in 2026.

**Credit Suisse: a push in size.** UBS paid USD 3.7bn in shares for net assets it valued at USD 31.6bn, a gain of USD 27.7bn, and its tangible book value per share has risen by two thirds since 2022. The return has not risen with it: 14.9% in 2022, 13.6% now. In theory it should be smooth sailing once the integration is done. One caution: profit has grown because integration costs fell, and that source gives out after 2027, the first year without them.

**The issue: what the two outcomes would cost shareholders.** UBS also inherited Credit Suisse's lawsuits and the government's scrutiny. About 3,000 holders are suing to reverse a USD 17.6bn write-off, for which UBS has set nothing aside. The upper house has voted for a capital rule that UBS says would take about USD 16bn of new equity at its parent bank, and far less counted the government's way. So fundamentally UBS is a strategic and sound bank that is performing well, and the issue is how these two outcomes would affect its shareholders, not its business. They would hold back shareholders' gains to add cushion to a bank that is already safe, and ask UBS's shareholders to pay for the Credit Suisse fiasco. Against that, the same takeover handed them a USD 27.7bn gain, more than the lawsuit's worst case. Neither outcome may come true, but both have to be considered: neither changes how UBS earns, and both decide how much of its earnings it must keep instead of returning.

## The variant view

**What the analysts' estimates hold.** Fifteen firms sent UBS their estimates on 7 October 2026, two weeks after the upper house's vote. Their average holds four things:

1. **The integration ends and the business grows on flat costs.** Integration costs fall from USD 2.1bn in 2026 to under USD 0.1bn from 2027. Revenue rises about 4% a year from 2026 to 2028 while running costs stay near USD 37bn. Profit reaches USD 14.6bn and the return on tangible equity 15.8%, from 13.6% today.
2. **Shareholders are paid most of it.** About 70% of each year's profit from 2027: a dividend rising from USD 1.26 a share to USD 1.59, and buybacks of USD 5.3bn in 2027 and USD 5.8bn in 2028.
3. **More capital than UBS's target.** The CET1 ratio rises from 14.6% to 15.2%, USD 6.3bn above UBS's 14% target by 2028, and buybacks stay below the USD 6bn of UBS's 2026 programmes while profit grows 31%. They do not state why.
4. **Litigation: about USD 0.2bn a year for every case together.** This is the line a lost lawsuit is charged to: what UBS sets aside for legal cases, less what it releases. The highest single estimate is USD 0.5bn. A settlement of the AT1 lawsuit alone is USD 10.5bn, and its worst case USD 17.5bn.

**How I price them.** I value a bank on its book: it is worth its book when it earns its cost of equity, the return shareholders ask of it, and more when it earns more. I take the analysts' 2028 return on tangible equity and tangible book, a cost of equity of 10.3%, which is their own average, and growth of 2% a year. Brought back to today, with the dividends paid on the way, their figures are worth USD 47.79 a share. The share closed at USD 47.42 on the day they were collected.

**Where I differ.** On the business, nowhere. I forecast no earnings of my own and take the analysts' profit, capital and shares as they stand. I differ on whether those gains flow back to shareholders or to the two events.

**The lawsuit: UBS can lose, and if it loses it pays.** UBS books a loss only once it is more likely than not, and has booked none; the estimates' line matches its books. I frame the lawsuit as a risk with two ends: everything stays as it is, or the hit is dire. So I price it at its odds (Pillar 1).

**The capital rule: it passes, and UBS uses the spare part of its buffer.** UBS's parent bank, the bank beneath the listed group, holds USD 10.6bn above its own target. I call that its buffer. UBS counts none of it towards the rule, and the government's count uses all of it. I think UBS still needs a cushion and keeps part of it, so my count of the new equity sits between theirs (Pillar 2).

**What cuts against it: the rule may be counted twice.** I take both costs off the analysts' figures in full. If the USD 6.3bn they hold above UBS's target is their allowance for the rule, part of the rule's cost, USD 1.13 a share on my odds, is already in their figures, and the lawsuit, USD 2.06 a share, is most of my difference with them.

## The case

**The three cases change what shareholders are paid, not what UBS earns.** Each works both events together, off the analysts' figures and out of the same buybacks. UBS returns the capital the group holds above a CET1 ratio of 14% as buybacks: USD 6bn in 2026, and I take the same for each later year. The rule comes first, because its schedule is the government's and UBS cannot defer it. The lawsuit takes what is left until its charge is rebuilt.

- **Best.** The Federal Supreme Court overturns the first ruling and parliament settles at the 75/25, 75 cents of equity to the dollar. The lawsuit costs nothing, and the rule takes about USD 0.7bn of each year's buybacks for five years.
- **Base.** Each event weighted by my odds. It is an average, not a path: UBS pays the lawsuit or it does not, so no single year will look like it.
- **Worst.** The holders win, every bond comes back, and parliament passes the 90/10. UBS books a loss in 2027, the year I take for the ruling, and pays no buybacks from 2027 to 2030. The lawsuit alone would stop them for three years. With the rule's share taken first it is four.

The buybacks and the book are not: in the worst case each share holds USD 4.75 less tangible book at the end of 2028, because the charge is gone and the shares were not retired.

Each cost is rounded on its own, so the two events can differ from their total by a cent.

**I hold: on my odds the two events cost about one year of payouts.** Neither event changes how UBS earns. Both take from what it pays out. The analysts have UBS paying its shareholders USD 9.5bn for 2027 and USD 10.3bn for 2028 in dividends and buybacks, 7% to 8% of the share's price a year. On my odds the two events take USD 9.7bn, once, and about twice that in my worst case. A year of payouts lost is a reason not to buy at this price. It is not a reason to sell.

**I buy below the price less the worst case.** The worst case takes USD 6.59 from a share, so at the close of 20 November 2026, USD 43.32, I buy below USD 36.73. That is my margin of safety, and it accounts for my worst case in full. Two outcomes would cost more: the government's own version of the rule, the whole dollar in equity, by about USD 0.60 a share, and UBS keeping its whole buffer, by about USD 1.15 (Pillar 2). If the base case comes instead I am USD 3.40 a share ahead, and in the best case USD 5.99.

**The buy price moves with the banks.** My rule takes the price as what a share is worth before the two events, but the price also moves for reasons that are not my two events. From 7 October, when the estimates were collected, to 20 November the share fell 9% and twelve banks fell 7% at the median. They are the ten large banks UBS is ranked against in The business, which with UBS make its eleven, and two wealth managers, Julius Baer and Raymond James. The ten are JPMorgan, Bank of America, Citigroup, Wells Fargo, Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, HSBC, Barclays, BNP Paribas and Deutsche Bank. A fall like that is common to the banks and is not a discount for my two events. So I move the USD 43.32 by the twelve banks' median change since 20 November before I take the worst case off.

**What cuts against my buy price: I might be counting the capital rule twice.** In September 2026 the share fell 11.5% in the two weeks the upper house passed the rule, while the Swiss market rose. If that fall was for the rule, I am counting it twice. The fall since then came with the other banks, and I put it down to the market outlook. So my buy price is a margin of safety, not what a share is worth. My view on the lawsuit still differs.

**The risk in my call is the economy.** I have done the work on the two events and not on the economy, so I do not forecast it: I rely on the share's price for it. If that price is too high for the economy that follows, my margin is smaller than USD 6.59, and after I buy the economy still moves the share: a tenth off the estimates' 2028 profit takes USD 5.19 from a share. If it is too low, the share may never fall to my buy price. A weaker economy also makes the events cost more: with less profit there is even less for shareholders, the buybacks stop for longer and the dividend may not be paid. I have not modelled that, so USD 6.59 is the worst case on the analysts' earnings and not in a weak economy. So I watch interest rates and the value of client assets (What its earnings ride on, above), the twelve banks' prices, and the analysts' estimates, which go through my model again each time UBS publishes them.

**Three events and two signs test my call.**

- **The National Council's vote,** expected in the winter session of 2026, decides the version of the rule (Pillar 2).
- **UBS's first capital plan after the final vote** decides how much of the buffer UBS uses (Pillar 2).
- **The Federal Supreme Court's ruling, then the remedy,** which settles what the holders get. No date is set for the ruling. UBS expects none before the first quarter of 2027, and I assume 2027 (Pillar 1).
- **Two signs each quarter.** The parent bank's CET1 ratio shows whether UBS is keeping the buffer. The pace of UBS's buybacks, which UBS ties to parliament's deliberations, is the other, starting with whether UBS buys the last USD 1.2bn of its 2026 programmes by its fourth-quarter results.

## Pillar 1: The AT1 lawsuit

**The verdict is uncertain, but UBS can lose it, so I price it in. Weighted by my odds it costs a share USD 2.06, about 5% of its price. If the Supreme Court upholds the first ruling the cost doubles, to USD 4.12, and if every bond then comes back it is USD 5.16. UBS can bear either loss. What shareholders lose is time: the buybacks stop for up to three years, the years in which the profit of the finished integration was to reach them.**

**The lawsuit: UBS inherited it with Credit Suisse.** In the 2023 rescue FINMA, the Swiss regulator, ordered Credit Suisse to write off all its AT1 bonds: CHF 15.8bn, the USD 17.6bn of The business at that year's rate. The holders sued, and on 1 October 2025 the first court found for them on all three questions before it: that they may sue, that the event the bonds' terms require had not occurred, and that the state could not order the write-off anyway. FINMA and UBS have appealed to the Federal Supreme Court, where they need to win only one. UBS is in the case as Credit Suisse's successor, with no indemnity from the state and nothing set aside.

**I frame it as two ends: everything stays as it is, or the hit is dire.** I give the holders 50 to win and 50 to lose. If they win, I split that half evenly again, between a settlement and every bond coming back.

| Outcome | My odds | UBS is charged | A share loses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The holders lose | 50% | Nothing | Nothing |
| The holders win, UBS settles at about 60 cents on the dollar | 25% | USD 10.5bn | USD 3.08 |
| The holders win, every bond comes back at its full face | 25% | USD 17.5bn | USD 5.16 |
| **Weighted by my odds** | | USD 7.0bn | **USD 2.06** |

**Why I take even odds.** I think the verdict could go either way. The holders have the first court behind them on all three questions, and Credit Suisse itself told FINMA on the day that the event the bonds' terms require had not occurred. But the Supreme Court has overturned this lower court before on the same kind of question, whether the law gave a sufficient basis: in the Credit Suisse bonus case, where the state cancelled managers' unpaid bonuses.

**If UBS loses, what it pays rests on three assumptions.**

1. **UBS pays, not the state.** The first court treated UBS as the losing party, and the Supreme Court kept the state out of the appeal.
2. **Every bond comes back, not only those of the holders who sued.** FINMA wrote down all 13 bonds by one decree, and the court struck down the decree's legal basis. The holders are back to where they were: they get their future coupons and have no claim to those already passed. Each bond is owed in its own currency and nine tenths are in dollars, so the bill is USD 17.5bn at the rates of 30 September 2026.
3. **A settlement costs about 60 cents on the dollar, USD 10.5bn.** The claims last traded at about 30 cents, in November 2025, up from 12 before the first ruling. At even odds that values a win at 60.

**How it reaches shareholders: the lawsuit soaks up the buybacks.** The charge lands whole on UBS's capital in the year of the ruling, and each dollar of it is a dollar of buybacks given up until the capital is rebuilt: 1.7 years of buybacks if UBS settles and 3.1 years if every bond comes back. UBS can bear either. From the 14.0% at which it expects to end 2026, the group's CET1 ratio falls to 11.9% in a settlement, above its requirement of 10.8%, and to 10.5% if every bond comes back, 0.3 points below, which restricts its payouts until kept profit lifts it back above. These figures are the lawsuit alone, on the day the charge lands. The 12.5% in The case's table is the end of 2027: after a year of buybacks kept, on the capital the estimates have UBS holding by then.

**The test.** The Supreme Court's ruling. If it upholds the first ruling, UBS is the party that pays, and its first results after it show how much: a provision of about USD 10.5bn says a settlement, and about USD 17.5bn says every bond back. If the court overturns the first ruling, the lawsuit leaves my worst case and my buy price rises by USD 5.16.

**What would prove me wrong.** UBS does not pay, or pays less than I price. The holders lose, which is half my own odds. The remedy falls on the state. Or only the holders who sued are restored: they hold at least CHF 5.5bn of the CHF 15.8bn, about a third, which cuts the worst case from USD 5.16 a share to about USD 1.89.

## Pillar 2: The capital rule

**The rule will pass, between the 75/25 and the 90/10, and UBS will find the equity out of its buybacks. Weighted by my odds it costs a share USD 1.13, about 3% of its price, and USD 1.42 in the 90/10, the likeliest version. There UBS has to find about USD 9bn. UBS says USD 16bn, and counted the government's way it is USD 4bn. Mine is the rise in what the bank must hold, less the USD 5.6bn of spare equity it already holds. What shareholders lose is about USD 1.9bn of each year's buybacks, from 2028 to 2032.**

**The rule: UBS backs its foreign subsidiaries with its own equity.** UBS's parent bank owns foreign subsidiaries worth USD 41.9bn, and the rule applies to USD 37.4bn of them once UBS has brought home what it plans to. Today the bank pays for less than half of each dollar with its own equity. A loss abroad then comes out of the cushion that protects the Swiss bank, which is how Credit Suisse's parent bank failed. I think the whole point of the bill is to make UBS accountable for its subsidiaries with equity, and the versions differ in how much. In the 90/10 I take the other 10 cents to be AT1, since the text sets nothing for them. The government wants full deduction, the whole dollar in equity, and UBS the 50/50. The upper house voted the 90/10 on 23 September 2026.

**Parliament settles between the 75/25 and the 90/10.**

| Version | Equity per USD 1 of subsidiary | My odds | Equity UBS finds | A share loses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 50/50 | 50 cents | 1% | None | USD 0.14 |
| 75/25 | 75 cents | 24% | USD 3.7bn | USD 0.60 |
| 80/20 | 80 cents | 25% | USD 5.6bn | USD 0.86 |
| 90/10 | 90 cents | 40% | USD 9.3bn | USD 1.42 |
| Full deduction | USD 1 | 10% | USD 13.1bn | USD 2.00 |
| **Weighted by my odds** | | | USD 7.3bn | **USD 1.13** |

**Why: the government will not pay for another Credit Suisse.** The bill is not new. It started after Credit Suisse fell, because Switzerland does not want to use its citizens' money to pay for losses made abroad. So I think it passes, with a concession. It will not be the 50/50: half of each dollar would be AT1, and AT1 does nothing to stop another Credit Suisse, whose AT1 could not be triggered while its capital ratio still looked strong. Full deduction asks too much of a bank Switzerland needs, and the upper house has already set it aside for the 90/10, the only version a chamber has passed. Reuters reported on 17 November 2026, citing three people familiar with the matter, that UBS is preparing to accept defeat in its effort to soften the rule and expects only token concessions.

**What UBS finds rests on the bank's buffer.** UBS counts none of the USD 10.6bn towards the rule. That is its USD 16bn, or USD 15bn on the balance sheet my model uses, that of 30 September 2026. The government's count uses all of it. I think the buffer was not meant for this rule and UBS still needs a cushion, so I split it in two.

1. **USD 5bn stays, against currency risk.** Currency is the one reason UBS gives for the buffer, and USD 5bn covers a fall of about 20% in the dollar.
2. **USD 5.6bn is spare, and goes towards the rule.** UBS states no purpose for it, and management is paid on return on CET1, so it has no reason to find new equity where it already holds some.

**How it reaches shareholders: UBS keeps buybacks, it issues no shares.** UBS finds the equity by keeping part of each year's buybacks. The government's schedule for its own version starts at 65 cents, which the bank's spare equity covers. I take that start, then five years to the level parliament sets. So in the 90/10 UBS keeps about USD 1.9bn of each year's USD 6bn of buybacks, from 2028 to 2032. The group's CET1 ratio rises from 14.0% to 15.9%, and the difference is equity UBS cannot return. The return on CET1 falls from 16.7% to 15.4%, the edge of UBS's target of around 15%. A dollar kept costs shareholders about 66 cents, not the dollar. The equity stays theirs, but it earns 4.8 cents a year in interest saved after tax where they ask 10.3.

**The tests.** The National Council's vote decides the version: the 75/25 raises my buy price by about USD 0.80, and full deduction costs more than my worst case and lowers it by about USD 0.60. UBS's first capital plan after the final vote decides the buffer. For the 90/10, about USD 9bn of new equity says UBS uses the spare USD 5.6bn, as I expect. About USD 15bn says it keeps the whole buffer, and the cost is USD 2.57 a share. About USD 4bn says it uses the whole buffer, and the cost is USD 0.58.

**What would prove me wrong.** The rule costs less than I price. UBS uses the whole buffer: the weighted cost is then USD 0.37 a share, and the capital the analysts already have UBS holding covers it. Or parliament passes the 50/50, or a referendum rejects the law.
